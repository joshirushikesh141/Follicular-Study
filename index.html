What is a follicular study?

Also called follicular monitoring or folliculometry. A doctor uses serial (usually transvaginal) ultrasounds during one menstrual cycle to watch follicles (fluid sacs in the ovary holding eggs) grow, and to time ovulation and intercourse/IUI. It is common with irregular cycles, PCOS, ovulation-induction medicines, or when trying to conceive for a while.

Typical process

  • Day 2–3: baseline scan – ovaries, resting follicles, ruling out cysts.
  • Day 8–10 onward: scans every 1–3 days to track the leading (dominant) follicle and uterine lining.
  • Mature follicle: about 18–24 mm. Growth is roughly 1.5–2 mm/day.
  • Lining (endometrium): ideally ≥ 7–8 mm, “triple-line” pattern.
  • Trigger injection (hCG): sometimes given to release the egg; ovulation follows in ~36–40 hours.
  • Confirmation: follicle disappears/collapses, a little free fluid appears; later a corpus luteum forms.

Pregnancy journey

Counted from the first day of your last period (LMP) – about 40 weeks.

Trimester 1 (weeks 1–13)

  • Fertilisation → implantation (~6–10 days after ovulation) → hCG rises; positive test ~ at missed period.
  • Heartbeat usually seen on ultrasound around 6–7 weeks.
  • Common: nausea, fatigue, breast tenderness, frequent urination.
  • Organs form; folic acid, prenatal vitamins, avoid alcohol/smoking.
  • Tests: dating scan (6–9 wks), blood tests, NT scan (11–14 wks).

Trimester 2 (weeks 14–27)

  • Energy often returns; bump shows.
  • Baby’s movements felt ~16–22 weeks.
  • Anomaly (detailed) scan at 18–22 weeks.
  • Glucose screening for gestational diabetes at 24–28 weeks.

Trimester 3 (weeks 28–40)

  • Rapid growth; backache, swelling, heartburn, Braxton Hicks contractions.
  • Growth scans, blood pressure checks, kick counts.
  • GBS swab ~36–37 weeks in many places; discuss birth plan.
  • Labour signs: regular painful contractions, water breaking, show.

Labour & after birth

  • Stages: early/active labour → pushing & birth → placenta delivery.
  • Options: vaginal birth or caesarean, as medically advised.
  • Postpartum: rest, feeding support, bleeding (lochia), mood changes, 6-week check.

🚩 Seek urgent care for

  • Heavy bleeding, severe abdominal pain, fluid leak
  • Reduced baby movements
  • Severe headache, vision changes, sudden swelling (pre-eclampsia signs)
  • Fever, persistent vomiting, thoughts of self-harm

What is an HSG?

Hysterosalpingography (HSG) is an X-ray test. Contrast dye is passed through the cervix so the doctor can see the inside of the uterus and whether the fallopian tubes are open. It is a key test in a fertility work-up.

Why it is done

  • Not conceiving after 6–12 months of trying
  • Suspected blocked tubes (past infection, endometriosis, surgery)
  • Recurrent miscarriage or suspected uterine shape problems
  • Looking for polyps, fibroids, adhesions or a septum
  • Before IUI or other fertility treatment

When and how

  • Timing: after bleeding stops and before ovulation (about day 7–12), so there is no pregnancy.
  • Procedure: speculum, thin catheter in the cervix, dye injected, X-ray images taken. The test takes about 5–10 minutes.
  • Feeling: period-like cramps; a painkiller beforehand is often advised.
  • Tell the doctor about possible pregnancy, iodine or dye allergy, or current pelvic infection or discharge.
  • After: light spotting and watery dye discharge for a day or two are normal.
  • Call your doctor for fever, severe pain or foul discharge.

Alternatives and risks

  • HyCoSy or saline sonography (ultrasound, no radiation)
  • Hysteroscopy and laparoscopy with dye test
  • Risks are uncommon: infection, dye allergy, small radiation dose
  • Not done during pregnancy or active pelvic infection

Before you conceive

  • Start folic acid (commonly 400 mcg daily; the dose is for your doctor to set) and a healthy diet
  • Aim for a healthy weight; stop smoking and alcohol
  • Check rubella and other vaccines, thyroid, diabetes and blood pressure
  • Review all medicines with your doctor

When to see a fertility doctor

  • Under 35: after 12 months of trying
  • 35 or older: after 6 months; over 40: sooner
  • Irregular or absent periods, PCOS, endometriosis, previous tube problems, or two or more miscarriages: seek advice early

Common fertility tests

  • Her: follicular study, AMH, thyroid (TSH), prolactin, HSG or other tube test
  • Partner: semen analysis (counts, movement, shape). Male factors cause about 1 in 3 cases.

Treatment steps

  • Lifestyle changes and timed intercourse
  • Ovulation induction tablets or injections with follicle monitoring
  • IUI: washed sperm placed in the uterus around ovulation
  • IVF/ICSI: eggs collected, fertilised in the lab, embryo transferred

Healthy pregnancy

  • Visits: monthly to 28 weeks, every 2 weeks to 36, then weekly
  • Food: protein, iron, calcium, fruit, vegetables, water; a small extra meal in trimesters 2–3 is enough
  • Weight gain: about 11–16 kg if starting at normal weight
  • Move: walk daily; sleep on your side later in pregnancy
  • Vaccines: flu and Tdap (usually 27–36 weeks), as your doctor advises
  • Avoid: unprescribed medicines, alcohol, smoking, raw foods
  • Mind: low mood or anxiety is common; talk to your doctor early

What is IUI?

Intrauterine insemination (IUI) places washed, concentrated sperm directly into the uterus around ovulation. It is a simple, low-cost first-line treatment and needs no anaesthesia.

Who may benefit

  • Unexplained infertility
  • Mild male factor (lower count or movement)
  • Ovulation problems such as PCOS
  • Cervical mucus problems, or use of donor sperm
  • Needs: at least one open fallopian tube (often checked by HSG) and a healthy uterus

Step by step

  1. Day 2–3: baseline scan and blood tests.
  2. Stimulation (optional): tablets or small-dose injections to grow 1–2 follicles.
  3. Follicular study: scans every 1–3 days from about day 9–10.
  4. Trigger: hCG injection when the leading follicle is about 18 mm or more.
  5. Sperm preparation: a fresh sample is washed in the lab.
  6. IUI: done about 24–36 hours after the trigger. It takes a few minutes and feels like a smear test. Rest 10–15 minutes afterwards.
  7. Support: progesterone is often prescribed for the second half of the cycle.
  8. Test: pregnancy test about 14 days later.

Chances and risks

  • Success is roughly 10–20% per cycle, and falls with age and cause. Doctors often suggest 3–4 cycles before moving to IVF.
  • Risks: twins or more with stimulation, mild cramps or spotting, rare infection, rare ovarian over-response.
  • Seek help for severe pain, fever, or heavy bleeding.

What is IVF?

In vitro fertilisation (IVF) collects eggs from the ovaries, fertilises them with sperm in the lab, and places one embryo in the uterus. ICSI is a version where a single sperm is injected into each egg.

Who may need it

  • Blocked or damaged fallopian tubes
  • Moderate or severe male factor
  • Endometriosis, low ovarian reserve, or older age
  • Failed IUI cycles, or genetic testing of embryos (PGT)

Step by step

  1. Work-up: hormone tests, AMH, scans, semen analysis.
  2. Stimulation: daily hormone injections for about 8–12 days, with scans and blood tests.
  3. Trigger: final injection to mature the eggs.
  4. Egg retrieval: about 34–36 hours later, under sedation, by ultrasound-guided needle. It takes 15–20 minutes.
  5. Fertilisation: standard IVF or ICSI in the lab.
  6. Embryo culture: grown for 3–5 days; the best ones are chosen, and extra ones can be frozen.
  7. Embryo transfer: a thin catheter places the embryo in the uterus. It is quick and usually painless. It may be fresh, or frozen in a later cycle.
  8. Support: progesterone for the lining.
  9. Result: blood hCG test about 9–14 days after transfer.

Chances and risks

  • Success depends mostly on the woman’s age and embryo quality. It is highest under 35 and declines with age; your clinic can give figures for your situation.
  • Risks: ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS), multiple pregnancy, ectopic pregnancy, bleeding or infection after retrieval.
  • Report severe bloating, fast weight gain, breathlessness or sharp pain right away.
  • The process can be emotionally tiring; support from your partner and counselling help.
